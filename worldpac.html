Minecart for Worldpac

Streamlining Worldpac field sales

Worldpac is not short on data. What it does not have is anything that sorts that data into a rep's next move. Minecart sits on top of Salesforce and does that sorting: which shops to be in this week, the two or three things worth raising when you walk in, and the signal managers need coming back the other way.

The problem

Roughly 120 TSMs cover shops across the US, Canada and Puerto Rico, and Worldpac holds more information about those accounts than almost anyone in the channel: Salesforce and the AS/400 underneath it, Zilliant money maps, Power BI, years of field call notes. None of it answers the question a rep asks at eight on a Tuesday morning: what should I do next.

For a TSM, a day built on habit

Nothing tells a rep which accounts need them this week, so the route runs the way it always has. Nothing tells them what to raise once they get there, so prep is tab-hunting in a parking lot or it does not happen. And what they learn in the shop goes into a notes field where it stops. The $1,000 turbo return is the version everyone remembers. The general case is quieter: a rep says the right thing in a note, and nothing picks it up.

Inside sales does not have this problem the same way. Zilliant tells an account manager where to look. The field has notes.

"If the AI was looking at the recovery and growth money maps here, that would be gold."
Worldpac field management, July 17

For a manager, slugging through data to find three things

Fifteen weekly summaries, field call reports a mile long, Agentforce surfacing whatever it has drifted toward. The harder version is connecting a push to what reps are hearing. You send the team after ACDelco consignments, and there is no clean way to see which accounts got the conversation, what came back, or whether it moved. Without that there is nothing to iterate on, so the next plan gets built like the last one, on instinct.

Competing priorities do the real damage

Consignments, strategics, collision, NVD, European coverage, and something new next month, because that is what a growing business looks like. Nobody should apologize for that. But a priority announced on Monday has a half-life of about a week, and when five are live at once a rep triages by relationship, by habit, by whichever account is closest to lunch. That is not a discipline problem. That is what anyone does with a queue nobody ranked. Then it compounds, because nobody can say which push worked, so nothing gets pruned and every cycle adds.

"Does the field team feel that this is a useful tool, and it's better than what we're doing today, and is there some sort of cost savings with that? And if all of that is true, then I think we may be able to do something."
Dominic Garcia, July 13

We took that as the rubric. Everything below is built so your own people can answer those three questions themselves.

Not everything. The right things.

Most tools that promise to help a field rep do it by giving the rep more. More dashboards, more panels, more KPIs, all arriving at once. The rep, standing in a parking lot with fifteen minutes before the next stop, scrolls. The job is the few things that change what a rep does in the next hour. Brake dollars down eight thousand against last year. A quote from nine days ago nobody followed up on. A delivery complaint the owner has not heard about yet. Three lines. That is the walk-in, and four things follow from it.

  • Who to visit. The week is grouped by geography and priority, so the accounts needing attention are the ones on the list.
  • What to raise. Two or three things per account before the visit, from sales performance, prior notes and the current push.
  • What they heard, back to the manager. Follow-ups with an owner, risks, and cross-account patterns a single call summary cannot see.
  • Feed it into the next plan. The part that answers iteration. Every note ties to an account and the campaign it touched, so you can ask which push reached which shops and what to run again. That record is also what keeps Salesforce current without anyone typing it.

How it works

For the TSM

See the day's accounts› Read the briefing› Talk for sixty seconds

That is the whole job. It runs in the browser extension beside Salesforce on the Surface, or on a phone, and it works offline in a shop with concrete walls. We log when a note was made and when it synced, so a batch uploading at 5pm in Puerto Rico still reads honestly.

For the manager

This is the answer to the half-life problem. A manager sets the default plan off sales performance, historical engagement, open issues and whatever criteria define the account set. Campaigns layer on top as overrides, and performance changes push accounts up on their own. When the focus shifts from consignments to strategics, the manager changes it once and every rep's day reflects it the next morning. A priority stops being an email somebody has to remember and becomes an account on Tuesday's route.

Structured fields, tasks and activity write back so the record stays current without re-keying, and the same data exports to Snowflake for the Power BI views your managers already live in. We are not asking you to move anything, and the investment you have made in Salesforce returns more, not less.

The recommended pilot

We have all watched what happens when a big platform lands on a field team in one motion. Trust out there is earned in small increments. So: two steps, each handing you something you can act on either way. Phase one proves we can tell a rep who to visit and what to raise, using managers who already know what a good day looks like. Phase two proves the rollup and the loop back into the next plan, which need real reps to mean anything.

Phase one

Managers plan their own field days

Why the analysis costs so little: planning a manager's day requires geocoding the accounts and loading your Salesforce data anyway. You are paying for the analysis and getting the pilot attached to it, rather than the other way around.

The outcome we are after is not a signature. It is an evidenced answer on whether Minecart supports your field team in 2027, shaped by what your own reps did rather than what a demo suggested. Both phases sit under one agreement with a gate between them, so there is one approval rather than two, and phase two only bills if phase one clears criteria agreed in writing beforehand.

What full implementation looks like

A pilot only means something if it de-risks a specific plan, so here is the plan.

  • Salesforce, connected properly. Notes push on submission rather than overnight, so a follow-up is actionable while the rep is still in the parking lot. The zero-copy ETL your team is onboarding is the cleanest path there. Your team sets what Minecart can see and write, field by field.
  • Tasks routed to one right owner. Credit to credit, pricing to the outbounder, delivery to branch ops. Once, not to three people. This is the piece you built on Einstein and set aside as too much custom development.
  • A data boundary Michael co-authors. Which objects and fields live in Salesforce and what stays in Minecart, specified rather than assumed.
  • Field intelligence into the warehouse. Structured data exports to Snowflake and lands in the Power BI views your managers and DC leadership already use.
  • Deployment across the field. Browser extension beside Salesforce on the Surface, mobile for reps who want it, about fifteen minutes of training each. Campaign and planning criteria configured per region.

Phase one settles the planning and criteria questions. Phase two settles the integration ones, at a scale where the answers hold. By the time this is a rollout decision the specification exists and has been tested rather than proposed.

What this is worth

Two numbers. The first is cash you can audit against your own expense reports today. The second is capacity, and it is much bigger.

The cash number: mileage

Re-clustering Luis Lucena's June returned 40% of his road miles. Where the assumption enters

The per-rep figure is measured. Everything above it is modeled from it, which assumes the pattern holds across other reps and territories. It might not, and some accounts genuinely cannot move days. That is the job: phase two replaces the modeled number with a measured one. If it comes back smaller you will hear it with the method attached, having spent $25,000 to find out rather than discovering it after a rollout.

How we would measure the win

Four things, all countable rather than felt.

  • Did mileage go down. Road miles per rep against their own prior months.
  • Did time come back. Hours in the truck, same comparison.
  • Did visits go up. Recovered time only matters if it turns into stops.
  • Did follow-ups get closed. Not captured, closed. A follow-up logged and ignored is the problem we started with.

Underneath those is the one we care about longest: did Worldpac end up with intelligence that stays when a rep moves on. And one honest note, because it is how we work. We will not get 100 percent of extractions right. Nobody does. We land around 80 percent out of the gate and tune from there with your team, which is why this starts small.

Outstanding questions

  1. The territory. Which one do we run phase one against? Ideally somewhere with real campaign activity and a mix of strong and struggling accounts.
  2. Ownership. Who owns this day to day, and who makes the call at the phase one readout?
  3. Success criteria. What would you add, cut, or raise the bar on? Better to agree now than argue in September.
  4. Day constraints. What share of accounts truly cannot move to a different day, because an owner is only in on Tuesdays or a delivery route dictates it? Your managers know this and no analysis of note data can tell us.
  5. Timing. What has to happen internally to start in August? Setup runs through August, use through September, readout in early October. A decision landing after the 2027 budget closes becomes a 2028 decision by default.

What would make the meeting a success

Appendix · Route efficiency case study

The same accounts, 40% less driving

One Worldpac TSM, one month, built from real Salesforce field-call notes. We geocoded every account Luis Lucena called on in June, anchored the routes at the Schaumburg branch, and asked one question: if that month were organized by geography instead of a fixed rotation, how much driving comes back?

The territory runs from Elgin and Schaumburg in the northwest down to Oak Lawn and Crestwood in the south suburbs, about 40 miles apart, and a fixed rotation puts far-apart towns on the same day. Holding the visits constant, the same 142 accounts across the same 15 field days, Minecart groups nearby accounts together and routes each day as a round trip from the branch. Every mile is a real road distance from the actual account coordinates.

The mileage is cash off the reimbursement line. The 13.7 hours is windshield time turned back into selling time, which is the number we would actually watch. This one rep is the unit the economics section above is built from, and phase one is where you check whether it holds across a territory.

How this was built

  • Source. 213 real June Salesforce field-call notes from one TSM, covering 142 accounts. Account names were resolved to coordinates via Google Places. No survey and no estimate.
  • Distance and time. Real road distances and driving durations from actual road-network routing, per leg, not straight lines. Every route is a round trip from the Schaumburg branch, so the commute is paid on both ends and nothing is gained by teleporting to the first stop.
  • Same visits. The re-organized month covers the same accounts in the same number of field days. Only which accounts share a day changes.
  • Conservative. The notes carry the day but not the visit order, so we routed even the actual days optimally. The real driving was longer, which makes 40% a floor rather than a headline. The Cleveland blitz week is excluded as non-territory travel.

The honest limit, and the question it raises

All of this assumes an account can move to a different day. Some cannot. An owner is only in on Tuesdays, a delivery route runs certain days, a shop asked for a standing time. We do not know how many accounts in this territory carry a real constraint like that, and it matters: if it is 15 percent, the number roughly holds. If it is 60 percent, it does not.

That is a genuine open question rather than a footnote, and it is one of the first things phase one would answer, because your managers know which accounts are actually fixed and no analysis of note data can tell us that.
