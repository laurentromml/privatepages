# Mining the transcript, voice, and the win-rate review

## Mining a transcript for voice of customer

The strongest proposals read like the client wrote the problem statement
themselves. Before drafting, pull these out of the transcript (Fathom or
Granola tools if you only have a link/ID) and keep them in a working list —
this material is the evidence backbone of the whole document:

- **Verbatim quotes, with name and date.** "I haven't seen a field report in
  two years." — Brian Rowland, CCO. Capture the exact sentence, who said it,
  and when. These go into "The problem" as evidence, attributed and (in web
  format) dated. Lightly trim filler only.
- **Their vocabulary.** The names they use for their own rituals and roles
  ("pre-flight", "triangle team", CAM/CPP/GM, "pulling teeth"). Adopt their
  vocabulary as the proposal's vocabulary — don't translate it into
  vendor-speak.
- **Past failed attempts.** What they already tried and how they describe
  the failure ("a very expensive tracking mechanism"). This becomes the
  differentiator section, in their words.
- **Pace and scope language.** "Crawl, walk, run", "baby steps", one region
  to start, one data source only. Honor the narrowing; never quietly
  re-expand scope.
- **Who's who.** Every stakeholder by full name and role — they populate the
  FROM/TO/RE memo block — and especially the approver who wasn't on the
  call. Get spellings right from the transcript, not from memory.
- **What was actually agreed.** Fees floated, term, phases, data access,
  next meeting dates. The proposal must match the call; flag any deliberate
  difference to Lauren.
- **Their calendar.** A real deadline in their world (Labor Day, a leadership
  meeting, a season start) to anchor the Next Steps section.

Keep this material in a **fact sheet with timestamps** as you mine: each
quote, name, tool, number, and agreement, with where in the transcript it
came from. This is not busywork. Lauren works many similar deals at once,
and the fastest way to lose her trust is a detail from one client's call
appearing in another client's proposal. The fact sheet is what lets you,
and her, prove that didn't happen.

## Quote handling rules

- Never invent a name, quote, or figure. Lauren hand-fixed fabricated
  attributions once; it can't happen again. If evidence is missing, leave a
  clearly-marked placeholder (`[QUOTE NEEDED — rep visibility pain]`) and
  tell Lauren, or do without.
- Quotes are evidence of the problem, not testimonials of the solution.
  Place them inside the problem narrative.
- Format: italicized, set off with space from surrounding prose, attributed
  with name + title (+ date in web format). Her last pass before sending AAP
  was exactly this: visually separating "the customer said this" from "we
  claim this."
- Illustrative examples that aren't real get the explicit label, verbatim
  pattern: "Beacon Ridge and Merrick Fastener Group are fictional, built to
  show the report format. The workflow is the one running today."
- Don't single out a named individual as the skeptic/problem in a doc that
  person may read ("Gunnar's objections" became "Your managers'
  objections").

## Voice and register

All prose through the **lauren-voice** skill, then a **stop-slop** pass.
On top of those, the proposal-specific rules:

**Two registers, chosen by format:**

- **Executive pre-read (Google Doc).** Formal-but-direct memo register.
  Contractions sparse ("do not" over "don't" is fine here). Colon-based
  apposition to define and expand: "A CPP's day is the counter and the
  phone, not a login." / "Setup is the real work: the account hierarchy, the
  report tuning, the CAM summaries."
- **Web proposal (HTML page).** Conversational-direct. Contractions freely.
  Short declarative fragments as emphasis: "We find them." "No forms."
  "That is not a discipline problem."

Both registers: second person throughout, active voice, a human subject
doing a concrete thing in every sentence, numbers always countable ("4,500
stores", "approx 500 CAMs", "a fifteen-minute walkthrough" — never "many" or
"significant"). No em-dashes, ever. Headers are plain labels or the reader's
literal question, sentence case, never clever.

**Context-accuracy beats voice.** A tight call-specific opening ("You asked
the right first question...") is wrong on an asset that will be reused with
people who weren't on that call. Ask what the document's real audience and
lifespan are before keeping call-specific framing.

**Proposal-specific AI tells** (these survived lauren-voice + stop-slop in
real drafts and got flagged, so hunt them explicitly):

- Quotable epigrams and snapped antitheses: "tested, not guessed",
  "context instead of memory". Say the plain version.
- Staccato mic-drops: "Notes. That's the whole job." / "Ever." A short
  sentence is fine; a short sentence posing for applause is the tell.
- Framing devices that admire the customer's quote before giving it
  ("you said something that should be printed on a poster"). Just give
  the quote.
- Performative candor: "we'll be straight with you about timing." Being
  straight is showing the honest date, not announcing straightness.
- The best de-slop trick in this corpus: when describing how the product
  works, reuse Lauren's own demo narration from the transcript. She
  already said it in her voice on the call; her phrasing beats fresh copy.

## The win-rate review (run before delivering, every time)

This checklist is what protects the 75% pilot win rate. Render/preview the
actual deliverable and check it, don't check the draft in your head.

**Substance**
1. Fee math consistent everywhere — memo RE line, summary table, investment
   prose, and tables agree; phase fees sum to the total.
2. Durations/hours/dates agree across intro lines, bullets, tables.
3. Scope matches what was agreed on the call; nothing silently re-expanded;
   every deliberate difference flagged to Lauren.
4. Names, titles, company spellings verified against the transcript. Title
   of the document spelled correctly (check it again — "Empowerd" and
   "Projet" both made it into real drafts).
5. No placeholder text, no internal/meta content, no unresolved "[TBD]"
   without Lauren knowing.

**Structure**
6. Guarantee/commitment section present. Next Steps present with a real
   calendar anchor. (These got merge-dropped from a real final. They are
   required.)
7. Pilot card has all three parts: validating / get back / need from you,
   and names the artifact the client keeps either way.
8. Success criteria in a Measure/Target table with a baseline plan and a
   report date.

**Credibility**
9. Every projected number has its hedge sentence nearby; every discount has
   its named reason; strikethrough anchor present.
10. Every pain claim carried by a real, attributed quote or clearly-labeled
    fictional example — nothing invented.
10b. **Cross-client contamination check.** List every company, product,
    tool, person, and number named in the draft. Each one must trace to
    (a) this client's transcript (cite the timestamp from your fact sheet),
    (b) canonical Tromml messaging, or (c) an other-client proof point that
    the text explicitly labels as another client's. Anything that fails all
    three gets cut. When delivering, give Lauren the receipts: a short
    trace list in chat (claim → timestamp) so she can spot-check without
    rereading the transcript.
11. Headcount-equivalent translation present in the ROI section if this is
    an executive audience.

**Voice**
12. Section-regression check: diff the product-description section against
    the best prior phrasing (a person, doing a thing, in a loop). If it
    reads like "unified intelligence layer" consultant copy, rewrite it.
    When assembling from multiple drafts, always merge from each section's
    most-recently-edited version — voice quality is not monotonically
    increasing across drafts.
13. stop-slop pass done after final assembly, not just on the first draft.
    Zero em-dashes (search for them).

**Format-specific**
14. HTML: after any cut, sweep for stranded visuals — a badge, callout, or
    image whose anchor text was removed. Check the page at phone width.
15. Google Doc: quotes italicized and set off; strikethrough pricing
    rendered; tables intact after conversion.
