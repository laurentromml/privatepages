# The proposal playbook

The spine, the canonical messaging, and the sizing rules. Everything here is
distilled from Lauren's shipped proposals (Auto-Wares final, Advance Auto
Parts, PowerStop, Worldpac, WHI) and — just as important — from what her
revision history shows she cut, fixed, or regretted.

## The spine

This is the section order her final drafts converged on, blended with the two
sections her best drafts carried that must not get lost. Keep the order; drop
sections the deal doesn't need (see Sizing, below); don't add new section
types without a reason.

1. **Title + memo block.** Title names the outcome, not the product
   ("Building an AI-Empowered Sales Force", "Reconnecting Store Ops to AAP
   Intelligence"). Lock the title early and proofread it every time it's
   touched — title typos were the most visible unpolished-draft signal in
   her drafts. Then a literal memo header — `FROM: / TO: / RE:` in caps —
   naming every real stakeholder, with the ask and price stated in one line
   in RE. This makes the doc read as addressed to a named committee, not a
   generic pitch. Build it from the actual attendee list.

2. **Summary table** (Doc/docx formats). Client, primary contacts, scope,
   term, investment, at a glance, before any prose. The approver reads this
   first.

3. **Project Summary.** Bold lead-in label ("**Executive summary.**" or the
   section header itself), then their situation in their numbers: "a 30%
   reduction in the field team over the last year, and 35 to 40% more
   accounts each." Open on the client's numbers, not an aphorism. Close by
   previewing what the pilot tests ("this pilot tests four things").

4. **The problem.** Name the client's own ritual in their own vocabulary
   (AAP's "pre-flight" and "triangle team") and show where it breaks. Every
   pain claim is carried by a verbatim, named, dated quote from their own
   people, placed here as evidence — not in a testimonial block at the end.
   If they've tried something before and it failed, say so in their words
   ("it became, in the team's own words, 'a very expensive tracking
   mechanism'") — their past failure is the differentiator, not something to
   dodge.

5. **The Minecart system.** Use the canonical product description (Lauren's
   own wording, August 2026): "Minecart reduces the friction of capturing
   field insights: reps take a voice memo and just talk. Our
   industry-trained AI attaches the note to the account, extracts the key
   intel and follow-ups, and tags the brand details." Then land the two or
   three outcomes that matter for this deal (leveled-up vendor/principal
   reports, effortless capture, a geographic planning view of accounts —
   distributors, jobbers, fleets). **Do not use the old "talks for about
   ninety seconds" framing — Lauren retired it.** Keep sentences concrete
   and active. **Warning: this is the section where consultant-slop
   reintroduces itself** ("unified intelligence layer", "simultaneously
   optimizing efficiency and impact", "administrative friction is
   eliminated"). If a sentence has no human subject doing a concrete
   action, rewrite it.

6. **Pilot details.** Small, countable, low-friction: name the district /
   region / rep count / user count ("one district, 10–12 stores, about
   twenty GM and CPP users"). State the friction removal outright: "Nobody
   changes how they work." Include onboarding mechanics (what data comes in,
   how long setup takes, and why it's fast if prior work exists). Use the
   three-part pilot-card grammar, each as its own labeled list:
   - **What we're validating** — the hypotheses
   - **What you get back** — the deliverables, including the one they keep
     either way
   - **What we need from you** — the asks, kept tiny, with the "No" list:
     "No hire (we bring the analyst). No IT project. No change to
     [their CRM], it stays your system of record."

7. **Success criteria.** A Measure/Target table — not prose, not bullets
   with inline targets (her drafts tried both; the table won). Baselined in
   weeks 1–2, reported on a named day (60 or 90). Pre-committed: "we report
   against these whether or not they land." Monitor, don't mandate: "we're
   not recommending mandating behavior... if it turns out the counter just
   wants to log in and leave notes, those are all buildable."

8. **ROI / the economics.** Show the arithmetic, don't just conclude it:
   baseline in round numbers, then each lever computed inline ("Raise the
   value of a visit 30% and that is $900,000 a year") so the reader can
   audit it. Translate percentages into headcount-equivalents for an
   executive audience: "a 20% increase in visits across the full 53-rep team
   is the field coverage of about ten additional reps, without hiring any of
   them." (Her final Auto-Wares draft dropped this sentence and was weaker
   for it — always include the translation.) And put an epistemic hedge
   right next to every projected number: "These are assumptions, not
   promises," "Where the assumption enters," "We land around 80 percent...
   and tune from there." The hedges read as credibility, not weakness, and
   they appear in every strong proposal she shipped.

9. **Investment.** Strikethrough anchor pricing, present in every draft of
   every deal: the standard price struck through next to the actual price,
   **always with the named reason for the discount** ("The difference is
   integration work that is already built: we connected and validated your
   sales data in 2025"). Never a discount without its reason. Three price
   layers, plainly, in one place: pilot flat fee / ongoing platform fee /
   at-scale per-seat or per-store range (a range is fine — precise enough to
   be credible, open enough to preserve room). In HTML, price and duration
   go in the pilot card's heading itself ("30 days of live activity,
   $5,000"), never buried in body text.

10. **Where this goes.** The expansion path if the pilot succeeds, and the
    strategic throughline (see Canonical messaging). Keep future work
    clearly optional and separately scoped.

11. **Our commitment.** Required. The champion gate + opt-out + keep-it-
    either-way combination: "We won't start the clock until the champion is
    named. If it isn't delivering value, you can stop. Everything captured
    is exportable and stays [Client] property, no matter what you decide."
    Handshake register, never SLA register ("we shake hands"). Every pilot
    names the standalone artifact the client keeps even if they walk away.

12. **Next steps.** Required. Three concrete asks anchored to a real date in
    the client's calendar, not a generic "let's discuss": "the district
    named, the export approved, and a date for first reports... live before
    Labor Day." / "We are holding the week of September 14 open."

13. **Agreement + provenance.** Signature block (Lauren McCullough, CEO,
    Tromml, Inc. / client name-and-title line), contact line
    (lauren@tromml.com). When the doc quotes call transcripts or shows
    illustrative material, close with the provenance footnote: quotes drawn
    from transcripts and checked against recordings, lightly trimmed for
    filler; illustrative diagrams/examples contain no client data. It
    answers "is this real?" before it's asked.

## Canonical messaging (keep these consistent, deal after deal)

These lines recur across every proposal. They are the consistent solution
story; vary the client material around them, not the lines themselves.

- **System of record vs. system of action.** "[Salesforce / Sales-i / their
  CRM] stays the system of record. Minecart is the system of action." Never
  position as rip-and-replace; always the layer that makes their existing
  investment return more.
- **The record-before-AI throughline.** "Every agentic system [Client]
  eventually builds will require this record, and it takes time to
  accumulate, which is why it starts now." This is the strategic urgency
  argument in every deal — the pilot is the start of the data asset, not a
  software trial.
- **The three measures.** Time saved for the individual, better visibility
  into what needs attention, long-term intelligence they keep. Every ROI
  story ties back to these.
- **Honest limits.** "We won't get 100 percent of extractions right. Nobody
  does. We land around 80 percent... and tune from there."
- **Augmentation, not replacement.** The tool does the reading, sorting and
  routing people do by hand today; it doesn't ask anyone to work a new
  system.
- **Objection-to-competitor AI** (when relevant): name it as a header
  question ("Why not just use Agentforce?") and answer on industry depth and
  action-vs-record, not on features.

## Rep-agency deal kit (numbers and terms Lauren reuses)

- **Pilot pricing.** Standard pilot range: $3,500 to $5,000, covering setup
  and usage. Discounts get one plain clause ("discounted for [Client] as a
  small repping agency"), never a paragraph of justification.
- **Pilot scope bullets** (adapt, don't pad): batch load of their CRM data;
  an initial account-activity analysis from that load, delivered as a
  keepable artifact; Minecart access for notes; monthly vendor reports
  after the first month closes, refined to their needs; pilot data
  available back for upload into their CRM; at no additional cost,
  consulting on the systems side and spec work on their planning process.
- **ROI benchmarks** (only ones Lauren has stated; never invent others):
  reps save about 2 to 4 hours a week of admin time; the vendor report
  process saves 1 to 2 hours per vendor (multiply by their line count);
  clients are seeing territory and line-coverage increases after their
  first vendor reports. The closer: one territory or line expansion pays
  for the project for the year.
- **Affiliate / move-forward benefits**: affiliate membership on
  continuing, retroactive credit for introductions made during the pilot,
  commissions of 5 to 10% paid in cash or applied to the monthly bill.
  Introductions supporting the VIPAR relationship are a priority.

## Sizing rules — match the document to the deal

Her shipped pages prove weight scales with stakes, so decide size first:

| Deal shape | Length & register | Proof | Objection handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small fast pilot (warm single buyer — PowerStop, Shimp) | Short memo, one scroll, one pricing card, **600–700 words of body copy max** | 1–2 named quotes only | Cut the defensive section entirely; end on the guarantee |
| Phased / multi-stakeholder deal ($15–25K+, committee — Worldpac, Auto-Wares) | Full spine, possibly an appendix case study | Named+dated quotes per sub-problem, measured stats | Strategic objection gets its own section with the objection as the header |
| Cold / exec briefing (no prior relationship — WHI) | Briefing register, more visual | Quotes + stat cards + logo trust strip + About Tromml | Competitor-AI section, sharpened |

Two more sizing rules from her edits:

- **Size the rebuttal to the size of the fear.** Existential worries
  (replacing their CRM) get a whole section. Tactical worries (IT
  deployment, new hardware, learning an app) get a clause woven into the
  existing sentence right where the worry occurs: "no app store, no MDM
  project and no new hardware."
- **Cut whole sections rather than trimming them.** When PowerStop's
  Salesforce-objection section stopped earning its place, Lauren deleted all
  six lines, not half of them. A section either earns its place or goes.

## Anti-patterns (things her revisions removed — don't generate them)

- Two documents in one (brief + proposal). One document, one register.
- Essay/aphorism headlines ("Why this is in front of you now"). Final drafts
  use plain category labels: "Project Summary", "Pilot Details",
  "Investment". Headers can also be the reader's literal question ("Why not
  just use Agentforce?", "What are we validating").
- Speculative "what we might find" tables with invented client specifics.
- Internal deal politics, prep questions, or notes to the champion left in
  the client document.
- Corporate verb-stacking: "improve rep productivity and effectiveness...
  maintaining strong customer relationships, addressing barriers, and
  capitalizing on opportunities" became "rep productivity is the constraint
  on holding customer relationships, clearing barriers, and working the
  opportunities already sitting in the book." One concrete claim beats three
  vague verbs.
- A discount with no reason, a stat with no hedge, a pilot with no
  keep-it-either-way artifact, a close with no date.
